Logo design and brand continuity

What it costs when your business looks like two companies · Vista, CA · August 2026

Your logo isn't your brand continuity. Continuity is whether every place a customer can find you agrees with every other place.

Same name, same number, same hours, same mark, same voice. When those things disagree, two separate problems start at once. Search engines stop trusting who you are, and people stop being sure you're one company. Different problems, different costs, and almost every article on this subject treats them as one thing.

Here's what it looked like on a real Vista business.

Five profiles, three logos, two names.

A print and apparel shop here in town, before we touched anything.

I pulled up every place they existed online: their website, Facebook, Instagram, Nextdoor, and a local listing on Friends of Vista.

Three different logo generations were live at once. Not variations of one mark. Different marks, from different years, sitting side by side. The cover photos were worse. The look on one profile had nothing to do with the look on the next. Check two of them before calling and you'd reasonably wonder if you'd found two companies with similar names.

Then the listing data:

  • Yelp had them under a shortened version of their business name while everywhere else used the full one.
  • Nextdoor carried a phone number that was simply wrong.
  • Operating hours disagreed across platforms.
  • Their portfolio images were old and watermarked with a third logo iteration from years back.

That last one is worth sitting with. They had retired that logo. It was still shipping, on every photo of their work, on every platform, indefinitely.

Finding one

Only two of those hurt the ranking.

This is where most advice on brand consistency goes soft, so I'll be specific.

The name mismatch and the wrong phone number hurt their search visibility. Business name, address and phone are what local search uses to decide that all these listings describe the same real business. When Yelp says one name and everything else says another, and one profile carries a number that reaches nobody, you've given the algorithm a reason to hesitate. Inconsistent hours are the same problem, smaller.

The three logos didn't hurt their ranking at all. Google doesn't compare your Instagram avatar to your website favicon. No crawler is scoring your cover photos against each other.

The logos were costing something else, and arguably something worse. Search gets you found. What happens in the next forty seconds decides whether anyone calls. A buyer who lands on Yelp, likes the reviews, then clicks through to a website that looks like a different business has been handed a reason to hesitate at the exact moment they were ready to act. You can't see that in an analytics dashboard. It shows up as traffic that doesn't convert, and most owners read it as a traffic problem.

So the name and the phone number get you found. The logos and the imagery get you hired. Both are worth doing, and they don't pay off the same way.

The work

What we fixed, in what order, and what it cost.

The order surprises people, so here's the reasoning.

Yelp came first. They have a lot of genuinely good reviews, and that Yelp page was outranking their own website. Their strongest search asset was a page they didn't own. When the property you don't control is the one people land on, that's the one you correct first, and fixing the name there resolved the biggest inconsistency in the set on the page getting the most eyes.

The Nextdoor phone number came second. A wrong number isn't an inconsistency, it's a dead end. Every day it sat there somebody dialed it and gave up.

Hours and address across every platform came third. Unglamorous and quick, and it's what makes the whole set agree.

Profile images, cover photos and bios came last. Every platform brought onto the current logo, with cover art from one visual system instead of four. While I was in each profile I rewrote the bios so they described the business the same way, in language that makes sense for someone searching in Vista or Carlsbad.

The audit took about two hours of analysis. The corrections took about four. At my rate of $85 an hour, that's six hours to make a business look and read like one company everywhere it appears.

That is profile and listing cleanup only. It is not the same job as a full local SEO audit, which also covers the website itself, structured data, and the on-page work, and runs closer to seventeen hours.

It's too early to claim a result and I'm not going to invent one. What's running now is an email campaign asking their happy customers for reviews, plus Google Business Profile posts that match what goes out on Nextdoor and Instagram, with the alt text field used properly on each. Their website is the next project. It needs technical and SEO work that listing cleanup can't touch.

Finding two

The other way continuity breaks: you only have a JPG.

A wellness practice in La Jolla came to me for a website redesign. When I asked for their logo files, a JPG was all that existed. No vector, no source file, nobody who still had one.

So I rebuilt it from scratch. Roughly two hours, and most of that wasn't drawing. The shape was simple. The work was identifying the exact typeface they'd used, from a flattened raster image, until I found the match. Then I built variations for different background colors and different placements, and sent them back packaged vector files so nobody would have to do this again.

This is the most common way a small business ends up with three logos, and it isn't a rebrand. It's a JPG. The sign shop needs a file, the embroiderer needs a file, the web person needs a file, and each one recreates it a little differently because nobody has the original. Three vendors, three near-misses, and five years on you have three logos and no idea which is current.

I changed how I work because of it. Now I do a full intake before a job starts, and if a logo needs editing or recreating I quote that time up front instead of absorbing it. It's a better conversation than it sounds. A client who finds out their logo doesn't exist as a real file is usually ready to talk about what else is missing.

The opinion

Your logo doesn't have to say what you do.

This is the correction I make most often, and it runs against the advice you'll find almost everywhere else.

Small business owners believe the logo has to communicate the business literally. HVAC company, so the logo has an air conditioner in it, or the letters HVAC. The instinct is understandable and it's usually wrong. Too on the nose, and you end up with a mark that looks like eleven other HVAC companies, because they all had the same instinct.

I had this exact conversation with a publishing company launching under the founders' own name. They wanted a rocket. Books, launch, liftoff. You can see the logic and it isn't stupid, it's just the first idea, and the first idea is the one everybody has.

What I built was a wordmark, with the mark drawing on books and elevation instead of depicting a rocket. Same idea, held one level up. It reads as a publisher without illustrating the word "launch," and it doesn't put them in a category with every startup that also liked rockets. The wordmark solved something practical too. For a company whose name is the asset, the name is what people need to remember, so the name is what the logo should make memorable.

Your logo doesn't have to explain your business. Your website does that, your Google listing does that, the words next to the logo do that. The logo's job is to be recognized and remembered. There are more interesting ways to signal what a company does than drawing the product, and the interesting way is the one that sticks.

The other opinion

Consistency is not sameness.

Here's the other thing I'd argue about with another designer.

Consistency gets treated as sacred, and sometimes you should break it. That's the whole reason logo variations exist. A wordmark, an icon, a stacked lockup, a single-color version, a reversed version for dark backgrounds. Those aren't decorations. They're one identity solving different problems.

Small business owners get nervous here and I understand why. They're attached to the main logo. It was expensive, or it was personal, and using anything else feels like a betrayal of it. So the one file goes on everything: the square profile photo where it renders four pixels tall, the dark website footer where a black mark disappears, the hoodie.

The hoodie is where I've watched this go wrong in the most literal way. A print shop in Carlsbad took a job from a customer whose logo was AI-generated and supplied as a low-quality JPG. The lines in it were far too thin. The customer wouldn't budge on using it as-is. It went to print and the lines broke on the garment. There's no version of that job where the shop wins. Print it and their work looks bad. Refuse it and they lose the customer.

The failure was baked in at the design stage. Thin lines are fine on a screen at full size. They're not fine at three inches on textured cotton, and a proper file set would have included a bolder single-color variation built for exactly that. Nobody made one, because nobody was thinking past the screen.

One logo everywhere isn't continuity. It's the thing that makes you look like a mom and pop shop. A system of variations, used deliberately, is what makes you look like a company somebody remembers.

So: the identity stays fixed, the expression flexes. Same colors, same type, same mark family, chosen correctly for the medium. If you have to change something to make it work in a given placement, that variation should already be in your file set. If it isn't, that's the gap to fill, and filling it is cheaper than a rebrand.

Where to start.

Ninety seconds, before you spend a dollar.

Open every profile you have in separate tabs. Website, Google Business Profile, Yelp, Facebook, Instagram, Nextdoor, any local directory you're listed in. Put them side by side.

Check four things:

  • Is the business name character for character identical everywhere.
  • Is the phone number the same, and does it ring.
  • Are the hours the same.
  • Is it the same logo.

Most people find at least two problems. Those two are usually costing more than the fix.
